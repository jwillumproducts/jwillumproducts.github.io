How to reconcile Stripe payouts into a bookkeeping journal (QuickBooks-ready)

Reconcile Stripe payouts in QuickBooks with a clearing account and a journal entry built from a Stripe export. Step-by-step guide.

Published 28 September 2026 · Updated 13 October 2026

Stripe deposits one lump sum into your bank, but that payout is really many charges, minus fees, minus refunds, sometimes minus a dispute. If you book the deposit as "sales", your income is understated, your fees disappear, and refunds get tangled in. Most bookkeepers fix this with a clearing account and a journal entry. This guide shows how to do it by hand from a Stripe export.

Why a clearing account works

Picture a holding account, usually called Stripe Clearing, that stands for "money Stripe is holding for me":

  • When a customer pays, gross sales go into clearing (debit clearing, credit sales).
  • Stripe's fee comes out of clearing (debit fees expense, credit clearing).
  • A refund comes out of clearing (debit refunds, credit clearing).
  • A payout moves money from clearing to your bank (debit bank, credit clearing).

If everything is recorded, clearing goes back toward zero each time a payout lands. Anything left over tells you what still needs a closer look.

Step 1: Set up five accounts once

In QuickBooks Online (or your ledger), create these accounts or map to ones you already have:

  1. Stripe Clearing: Other Current Asset (or a clearing/bank-type account)
  2. Stripe Sales: Income
  3. Stripe Fees: Expense
  4. Stripe Refunds: contra-income or expense (pick one policy and stick to it)
  5. Bank Account: the real account that receives Stripe payouts

Write the names down exactly as they appear. Journal CSV imports usually fail on spelling or capitalization mismatches.

Step 2: Export the right Stripe file

In the Stripe Dashboard, go to Balances, open the transactions view and Export. Choose the itemized balance transactions export, not the payments list. You want columns like created, amount, fee, net, type or reporting_category, description and payout (or automatic_payout_id).

Export the date range for the month you're closing. Check whether amounts are in major units (100.00) or cents (10000). Mixing the two is the classic "everything is 100× too big" mistake.

Step 3: Categorize each row

Sort or filter by reporting_category (or type if that column is missing) and group rows:

Category Journal lines
charge / payment Dr Clearing (gross), Cr Sales (gross); then Dr Fees, Cr Clearing (fee)
refund Dr Refunds, Cr Clearing
stripe fee Dr Fees, Cr Clearing
payout Dr Bank, Cr Clearing
payout reversal the reverse of a payout
dispute treat like a refund, and note it as a dispute

Rows such as internal transfers or adjustments don't fit these buckets. Set them aside and decide on each one with your bookkeeper rather than forcing them in.

Step 4: Build one journal per day

Use a pivot table or SUMIFS to total each category per calendar day. Then write a journal entry for each day with the columns most CSV importers expect:

Journal No., Journal Date, Account Name, Description, Debits, Credits

A readable numbering scheme like SC-2026-03-15 makes entries easy to find later. Check that total debits equal total credits for every journal before you import. One unbalanced day blocks the whole import.

Step 5: Tie out the clearing account

After import, look at the Stripe Clearing balance for the period. If it isn't zero (or equal to charges that haven't been paid out yet), the usual suspects are:

  • date-range cutoffs (charges at month-end paid out next month)
  • rows you set aside (transfers, adjustments)
  • mixed currencies
  • a cents vs. major units mix-up

Compare your category totals to the Stripe Dashboard for the same range and you'll usually find the gap within minutes.
